Simplifying finance

Upstox was built for traders. When we saw how many people wanted to invest for the long term, we built a separate mode for them: Upstox Invest, a place to build the habit of investing regularly and grow a portfolio over time.

Its users ranged from first-time investors to experienced ones, so every screen had to work for both. One rule shaped them all: show the risk alongside the return, so people understand the downside before they commit their money.

Same app, two very different people

Upstox's existing users were traders. The people we wanted to bring into Invest Mode were investors. They came to the app with different goals, different patience and very different levels of financial knowledge.

THE TRADER

“Show me everything, and let me act fast.”

MINDSET

In a hurry to catch a price point and place the next trade.

ON SCREEN

Dense pages and fast flows. Delight isn't a priority.

KNOWLEDGE

Already fluent in the terms, KPIs and metrics.

THE INVESTOR

“Help me understand it before I put money in.”

MINDSET

Making a long-term, informed bet. Small fluctuations don't matter.

ON SCREEN

Familiar formats, like shopping, delivery and cab-booking apps.

KNOWLEDGE

Anywhere from beginner to expert. Needs metrics shown simply.

WHAT THIS MEANT FOR DESIGN

Invest Mode couldn't be the trading app with a new tab. It needed its own patterns: familiar layouts, plain-language metrics, and risk shown alongside return, so anyone could make an informed decision.

Section 01

Stocks

Buying a stock should feel like buying anything else

Most stock pages are built for traders, with candlestick charts, raw volume figures and jargon that assumes you can read a chart. But people opening a stock in Invest Mode weren’t trading. They were deciding whether to buy.

What we built: a stock page that reads like a shopping app, not a trading terminal. We borrowed the signals people already trust when they shop online:

  • Ratings: analyst views shown the way people read product reviews.
  • Social proof: how many people follow the stock, and whether it makes lists like “top in its sector” or “most traded this week.”
  • Plain numbers: how many people bought and sold today, instead of a raw volume figure.
  • A checklist: the stock’s key health signals at a glance, beside one simple price graph instead of a wall of charts.

Section 02

Mutual funds

Risk and returns, in words anyone can read

Most fund pages show risk and returns in a format built for professionals: alpha, beta, standard deviation. A regular investor can’t tell from those whether a fund is good, or right for them.

What we built:

  • Plain names: alpha became “Excess returns over index,” standard deviation became “Consistent returns,” and downside capture became “Downside protection.” Each has one line saying what it means and whether higher is better.
  • Context, not just numbers: each metric sits on a scale against other funds in the same category. Most people see a simple verdict, like “High return vs category.” Those who want more can open any metric to see exactly where the fund sits.

Over time, this teaches people which numbers matter when choosing a fund.

Section 03

IPO (initial public offering)

The 300-page prospectus nobody opens

Most apps show IPO subscription numbers, but those say little about whether the company is any good. The real signals sit in the red herring prospectus (RHP), which almost nobody reads: revenue, profit, debt, valuation, 3-year performance, how the company compares with its sector, and what the money raised will be used for.

What we built:

  • The prospectus as visuals: revenue, profit and debt appear next to the sector average and the sector leaders, so people can see whether a number is good, not just what it is. How the money raised will be used (expansion, debt repayment, general use) is shown as a simple breakdown instead of a dense table.
  • Tips for oversubscribed IPOs: apply once, for a single lot, at the cut-off price. These small changes improve the odds of getting shares, but most first-time applicants don’t know them.

Section 04

Fixed deposits

“Safe as an FD” isn't always true

For most Indians, a fixed deposit is the safest place for money: it’s what our parents trusted. Then newer NBFCs (non-banking finance companies) began offering FDs at much higher rates. People saw a familiar product with a better rate and assumed it was just as safe. Most didn’t know that bank FDs are insured, but NBFC deposits are not.

What we built: safety shown right next to the rate. Every FD is clearly marked as either insured by DICGC, the RBI body that protects bank deposits up to ₹5 lakh, or not insured. People can now weigh a higher rate against the safety of their money before they invest, not after.

The rule held everywhere

Four asset types, four different sets of jargon, one test applied to each screen.
