Prime Video: Localization Central

Every Prime Video title has to be localized before it can launch in its 26 main markets: subtitles, dubbing, audio description, compliance cuts and metadata, for every language and region.

Local vendors do this work, so each launch means sharing, tracking and receiving assets across dozens of external teams, all in the short window between production wrapping and release.

Localization Central is the web tool I designed to share, track and receive assets with every local vendor in one place.

Step 01

One system, two modes

One tracker for Amazon and every vendor

Instead of separate tools for each side, I designed one unified system with two modes. Amazon mode is where the localization team plans titles and sends out source files. Vendor mode is where localization service providers are onboarded and see only the work assigned to them.

Both modes share one tracker, so everyone sees the same status. Assets are shared cleanly with access controls, and any change triggers one notification that shows its downstream effects, so vendors know exactly which assets to redo. The system connects to the tools on either side, from the Studios calendar to DAMs and Cloud Courier, so files move safely without leaving the system.

Step 02

Information architecture

Built around how the team already tracked the work

I didn't ask the team to rework how they tracked titles. Projects became the main pivot, with every language, workstream and asset sitting under them.

Each project opens on an overview with a Gantt chart, because that's how the team already planned in Excel, next to a list of every asset. Together they show changes before they cascade and put a delivery date at risk.

Every level shows anomalies upfront, so issues are spotted and acted on faster. Every asset also has a full version history, so during an escalation the team can see exactly what changed, when, and by whom.

Step 03

Design system

Don't reinvent the wheel

With two releases to ship in three months, I started from Amazon's existing design systems instead of building a new one. Proven patterns like the masthead, search and form fields came across as they were, and I adapted a few components to fit how localization teams worked.

I only built from scratch what the work demanded: the asset status cards, the WPR status tags and the project header. That kept the UI familiar to Amazon users and let us move fast without hitches.

Step 04

Usability testing

Rating every step, not just every task

I ran qualitative sessions with 10 users, done remotely because of COVID. Each person worked through a set of tasks covering both happy paths and error cases, then rated each task as "Completed easily", "Completed with effort" or "Could not complete".

I then broke every task into its steps and rated each step on the same scale as a moderator. Lining up the users' ratings with mine made the patterns obvious: exactly where people struggled or got stuck.

The gap between users' ratings and mine was the most useful signal, because it showed where a step felt harder than it was, or was harder than people realised. Those gaps shaped our training modules, help content and feature updates. Bugs went into the next release's backlog, and new requests went to the product backlog for triage.

✦What I learned
01Adapt to the circumstances. The project started in December 2019. When the pandemic hit, we moved reviews and testing online and kept shipping, slower but steady.
02Integration is never ideal. Working with other systems, plans change. I learned to keep the intent of a feature fixed and the UX flexible.
03Prioritize ruthlessly with data. Stakeholders wanted every frill. Data showed what would move the needle, and shipping the basics early got us real feedback faster.
