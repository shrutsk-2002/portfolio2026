Account opening - Improving the funnel

Every investor in India needs a Demat account (the electronic account that holds their shares) before they can trade. Ours was a rigid, fixed-order flow stacked with third-party integrations and validation checks — and fewer than one in four people who started it finished. We rebuilt it as a modular flow: fewer errors, more completions, and a product team free to test the sequence itself.

Problem 01

Get more people through the flow.

Flow completion rate

24% → 32%

Finding where people dropped off

Completion had been sliding for months. We ruled out the obvious suspects first — market conditions, a failing integration — and found no pattern. Product wanted to test a different step order, but the way the flow was built made that impossible. So we walked it step by step and mapped every error state and drop-off point.

The failures fell into two groups.

Data mismatches: small errors, outright rejections

People entered a name, signature or place of birth that didn't exactly match their official documents, or took selfies that failed verification. Each mismatch meant a rejection, and people rarely understood why.

What we built: visual references to match against. We showed the PAN card (India's tax ID card) exactly as it would appear, so people could match details and signature as shown on the card to prevent errors.

Every new account needs a liveness check: a selfie matched against the photo on the person’s official documents. This step had one of the highest rejection rates in the flow. Selfies failed because of poor lighting, covered faces, glasses, or someone else in the frame.

We explained the rules in text before the camera opened. People skimmed it, took the selfie anyway, and were rejected without knowing why.

What we built: a short looping animation, shown right before the camera opens, of selfies that fail and selfies that pass. People understood the rules in seconds, and more got it right on the first try.

Information-heavy steps: asking for what people didn't have to hand

Linking a bank account meant recalling an account number and IFSC code (the bank branch's routing code) — details few people have within reach. A small ask, and a heavy drop-off.

What we built: we automated the step with an external service that pulls bank details from a phone number. Instead of typing, people just confirm what's been fetched.

That alone removed most of the manual-entry errors at this step.

Problem 02

Get more people activated for F&O.

F&O segment activation

5% → 10.5%

Proving eligibility without the paperwork

The old flow made F&O feel like an extra step, so most people skipped it. Yet after their first order, many came to support asking how to activate it. The demand was there; we were asking at the wrong moment. So we moved activation into account opening.

SEBI, India’s market regulator, requires a bank statement showing a minimum balance over a specific period. People often uploaded the wrong period or an account without enough balance, and were almost always rejected.

What we built: an automatic fetch through the Account Aggregator. With the person’s consent, the statement comes straight from their bank and is validated on the spot: the account is active, belongs to them and covers the required period. People see approval on screen instead of waiting for a manual review.

Problem 03

Design for experimentation.

Post-redesign

Product runs experiments without waiting on design

One flow, any sequence.

The flow was strictly linear, and product kept hitting the same wall. They needed to add a manual-entry screen when an integration failed, switch off steps in volatile markets, or simply test which order converted better. The design couldn't support any of it.

What we built came in two parts.

A modular page layout — one header and footer shared by every step, around a flexible content area. Engineering could drop any step's content into it, so pages were reused instead of redesigned each time the sequence changed.

An index screen — showing people where they are and how much is left. It also does cognitive work: the flow runs at least eight steps, but the index groups them into three stages, giving people an anchor and making the whole thing feel lighter than it is.

How we launched

A phased rollout, so problems surfaced at 1% instead of 100%.

What I'd do differently

  • Lean harder on the bank integration — it already returned name, father's name, PAN details and bank linking, yet people still typed most of it in. It could have been step one, turning everything after it into review-and-approve. We held back because its error rate was high, and building on it would have brought back the sequential dependency the redesign set out to remove.
  • Test in a closed sandbox first — this flow drives the funnel and the revenue behind it, and the change was drastic; upfront testing would likely have caught UX issues before they shipped. Given the timeline, we chose A/B testing in production over a 2–3 week delay.
  • Bring integration owners in earlier — the bank-statement and penny-drop failures were foreseeable, since both depended on third parties. Involving those teams during design, not after launch, would have surfaced them before rollout instead of during it.
