Pixel: automating Prime Video's marketing artwork

Prime Video was launching in new markets faster than marketing could make artwork. One title needed over 2 million assets.

Twelve design teams built them by hand, covering under 1% of the catalogue in fewer than a third of Prime Video's languages. Only tentpole titles got full marketing, with little room to experiment.

I designed Pixel to automate asset creation, so marketing could reach every title, not just tentpoles.

Step 01

User research

Twelve teams, twelve ways of working

I followed artwork requests from brief to delivery with all 12 graphic design teams, plus merchandisers and marketing.

Every team had its own workflow. Some kept master files per market, others rebuilt layouts each time. Their edge cases, like right-to-left languages, local logos, regional art and last-minute price changes, became the rules Pixel had to handle.

The pattern: most of the effort went into repeating work, not design. Resizing, translating and re-exporting left almost no time for new ideas.

Step 02

Information architecture

01

Break every asset into building blocks

Using what I learned from the teams, I mapped each asset’s structure into four levels, so teams could edit at any level with minimal work:

Elements: title logos, the Amazon logo, price tags

Placements: sizes like desktop, TV, mobile and Facebook ads

Templates: layouts that combine elements for a placement

Graphic sets: all of the above, plus a campaign’s marketing logic

02

Give the logic one home

Each element is defined centrally, with its rules and how it relates to the others. One marketplace might need a title logo in 7 languages, dark and light; another needs its own pricing plans and release dates. Teams set and change these rules themselves, without going back to design or engineering.

03

Manage the work, not just the assets

I designed the workflow around how marketing teams operate:

Assign, review, approve. Work is assigned to an owner, and nothing goes live without a maker-checker review.

Role-based access. Each person sees and acts only on what they own.

Change requests. Teams raise and track changes in the tool, not over email.

Notifications. Requests, approvals and deliveries move without anyone chasing them.

Step 03

Building the UI

Intuitive where it mattered, familiar everywhere else

My rule was simple: make the UI intuitive where the work was new, and don't reinvent the wheel anywhere else.

Most of the setup was entering metadata for each element, so I used simple, familiar form fields. Where the content had layers, like a title logo with its languages and light and dark versions, I built a tree structure so designers could see how everything connected before they uploaded anything.

For building templates, I copied the patterns of the vector tools designers already used every day: layers, artboards and properties panels. Nobody had to learn a new way of working.

I also added checks that flagged errors as they happened. If a change broke the hierarchy, or a logo variant would spill outside a placement's safe space, the system warned the designer before the asset ever went out.

Step 04

Photoshop integration

Don't fight Photoshop, work with it

Throughout the build, one question kept coming up: can designers really design in this? Am I building Photoshop all over again?

The honest answer was no. Photoshop has decades of advanced features, and I had ten weeks to launch. I could never catch up, and designers would never want to give it up.

So instead of competing, I integrated. I built a Photoshop plugin where a designer could paste a unique link from Pixel and import every artboard, with its templates and placements already set up. They could work and experiment freely in a tool they knew, then push the file back to Pixel, where it was automatically replicated across every variant.

Next steps when I moved on

  1. 01Connect straight to the catalogue. Finished assets were saved to a digital asset management (DAM) system, then moved into Prime Video by hand. The next step was a direct link, so assets flow to wherever they’re used across Amazon automatically.
  2. 02Reach beyond Amazon. Integrate with external DAM systems, so partners and outside teams get assets the same way.
  3. 03Refine the interface. Launch brought feedback on the UI, bugs and flow, which we picked up in the next release.

What I learned.

  1. 01 Work with Photoshop, not against it. Photoshop is far more powerful, so I didn't compete. Pixel accepts Photoshop files and integrates with it, so advanced edits stay in Photoshop and simple ones happen in Pixel.
  2. 02 Simplify, don't replicate. Designers' existing process was shaped by its limits, not by what was efficient. Copying it into a tool would have kept those limits, so I looked for the simpler path underneath.
  3. 03 Start small. I launched on low-visibility titles with four template sets, validated the output, then scaled.
  4. 04 Build for what comes next. The same structure now supports retrospective updates, video content and personalised assets.
